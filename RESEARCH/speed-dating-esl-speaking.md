# ESL Speed Dating with Cue Cards — Research Findings

## Overview

Classroom speed dating is a timed, rotational speaking activity in which students hold a series of short one-on-one conversations with different partners. The format is adapted from social speed dating: pairs face each other, talk for a fixed time (typically 2–5 minutes), then rotate to a new partner. Cue cards scaffold the interaction with questions, prompts, or role information.

Sources consulted: eslactive.com, theesleducator.com, writeonwithmissg.com, ideasforfrenchclass.blogspot.com, Facebook ESL teacher groups, Instagram (@somewhere.to.share), Wordwall, Harvard ABLConnect, and Scribd activity guides.

---

## 1. Procedure

### Setup
1. **Arrange seating** in two facing rows (inner/outer circle or parallel lines), so each student faces one partner. A line of tables between them adds realism.
2. **Prepare cue cards** — two kinds depending on the variant:
   - **Question cards**: 5–8 conversation starter questions printed on a card placed between each pair.
   - **Profile cards**: invented character details (name, age, nationality, job, hobbies, personality) — one per student or drawn from a hat.
3. **Explain the format**: students will talk for a set time, then rotate on a signal (bell, timer, hand clap).

### Execution
1. **Round 1**: Pairs converse using the cue card as a guide. Student A asks, Student B answers (or both take turns).
2. **Rotation**: On the signal, one row shifts one seat to the right (end person moves to the front). New pairs form.
3. **Repeat** for 5–10 rounds, depending on class size and time available.
4. **Optional role swap**: Midway through, interviewers and responders switch roles.
5. **Follow-up**: Pairs report what they learned, or do a written reflection (e.g., reported speech, summary).

### Timing
- 2–5 minutes per round
- 10–20 minutes total activity time
- One full class period for extended versions (with costume, role-swap, and debrief)

## 2. Variants

| Variant | Cue Card Content | Language Focus |
|---------|-----------------|----------------|
| **Icebreaker / Get to Know You** | Personal questions (hobbies, pets, family, home, opinions) | Present simple, question forms |
| **Character Roleplay** | Profile cards with invented personas (Jack Sparrow, Yoda, etc.) + costumes/hats | 3rd person, describing people |
| **Vocabulary Introduction** | Topic-specific questions with target vocabulary and answer starters for low levels | Topic lexis, full-sentence answers |
| **Literary Analysis** | Questions about setting, characters, themes, plot, conflict from a novel | Analytical discussion, evidence |
| **Speed Debating** | Debatable prompts (silly to serious) | Persuasion, rhetorical appeals |
| **Presentation Practice** | Students present their work to one partner at a time, receiving feedback | Presentation skills, Q&A |
| **Peer Feedback** | Focused rubric sections (hook, thesis, topic sentence) — one per round | Writing revision, metalanguage |
| **Gap Fill / Info Exchange** | Student A has information Student B needs (e.g., picture description) | Questions, listening for detail |

## 3. Justification / Pedagogical Rationale

### Affective benefits
- **Low-pressure intimacy**: Speaking to one person at close range is less intimidating than presenting to the whole class. "It's both intimate and anonymous" (The ESL Educator).
- **Anxiety reduction**: Short rounds limit performance pressure; mistakes feel less consequential.
- **Confidence building**: Shy and reluctant students participate because the format is structured and predictable.

### Fluency development
- **Repetition with variety**: Students answer the same questions multiple times, which automatises vocabulary retrieval and grammatical accuracy.
- **Extended speaking time**: Every student speaks for nearly the entire activity — no one is passively listening.
- **Authentic interaction**: Conversations are real (not read-aloud) — students listen, react, and adapt.

### Cognitive and linguistic benefits
- **Quick thinking**: The time limit forces students to retrieve language rapidly without pre-planning.
- **Noticing and scaffolding**: Weaker students hear stronger peers model answers and absorb vocabulary and sentence structures.
- **Multiple exposures**: Hearing 6–10 different answers to the same question reinforces target language.

### Classroom management
- **100% engagement**: Every student is actively speaking simultaneously. "This engages every single student at the exact same time" (Write on With Miss G).
- **Teacher as observer**: The teacher circulates and listens — ideal for formative assessment and on-the-spot error correction notes.

### Theoretical alignment
- **Informal cooperative learning** (Johnson & Johnson): Short-term, ad-hoc groups working toward joint language production goals.
- **Task-based language teaching**: The task (complete short conversations with multiple partners) drives language use.
- **Krashen's Affective Filter**: Low-anxiety setting lowers the filter and promotes acquisition.

## 4. Examples of Cue Cards

### Example A — Icebreaker / Personal Information (Beginner–Intermediate)
```
CUE CARD — ROUND 1: GETTING TO KNOW YOU
───────────────────────────────────────────
1. What's your name? (or: What's your character's name?)
2. How old are you?
3. Where are you from?
4. What do you do? (job / student)
5. What are your hobbies?
6. Describe your personality in one word.
7. What kind of music do you like?
```

### Example B — Vocabulary Introduction: "Travel" (Intermediate)
```
CUE CARD — ROUND 3: TRAVEL
───────────────────────────────────────────
1. Have you ever been to another country? Which one?
2. Do you prefer beach holidays or city breaks? Why?
3. What's the most interesting food you've tried while travelling?
4. What three things do you always pack in your suitcase?
5. Would you rather travel alone or with friends? Explain.
```

### Example C — Character Profile Card (Roleplay)
```
PROFILE CARD
───────────────────────────────────────────
NAME: Captain Jack Sparrow
AGE: 40-ish
NATIONALITY: British (but loyal to no flag)
JOB: Pirate captain
HOBBIES: Navigating, negotiating, escaping death
PERSONALITY: Clever, unpredictable, selfish but charming
LIKES: Rum, freedom, the Black Pearl
DISLIKES: The Kraken, Davy Jones, authority
```

### Example D — Speed Debate Card (Advanced)
```
DEBATE CARD — ROUND 4
───────────────────────────────────────────
TOPIC: Social media does more harm than good.
YOUR POSITION: Agree (even if you disagree IRL)

Points you could make:
  • Mental health and comparison culture
  • Spread of misinformation
  • Reduced face-to-face interaction
  • Addiction and screen time

Use at least one rhetorical question.
```

### Example E — Low-Level + Answer Starters (A1–A2)
```
CUE CARD — MY FAMILY
───────────────────────────────────────────
          Question                      Answer Starter
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1. How many people are in your family?  There are ___ people in my family.
2. Do you have brothers or sisters?     I have ___ brother(s) / sister(s).
3. Who is the oldest in your family?    The oldest is my ___.
4. What does your mother/father do?     My mother/father is a ___.
5. Do you live with your grandparents?  Yes, I do. / No, I don't.
```

## 5. Key Design Principles

1. **Cue cards must match the target language and level.** Lower levels need simpler questions and answer starters; higher levels benefit from open-ended prompts and opinion-based questions.
2. **Rotations work best with 5–10 partners.** Fewer than 5 reduces repetition benefit; more than 10 causes fatigue.
3. **The teacher circulates and listens**, not interrupts. Collect common errors for a delayed feedback board after the activity.
4. **Follow-up consolidates learning.** Have students write 3 things they learned about classmates, or report one partner's answer using reported speech.
5. **Props and costumes boost engagement**, especially with younger learners and character roleplay variants.
